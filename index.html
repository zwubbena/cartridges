Part 1

One round, four parts

A loaded round is a cartridge. The bullet is only the part that leaves the barrel. Pick a part to see what it does.

Where the primer sits

Centerfire: the primer is a separate cup pressed into the center of the base. Nearly every handgun and rifle cartridge in this guide. Cases can be reloaded.

Rimfire: the priming compound is spun into the hollow rim, and the firing pin crushes the rim edge. The .22 LR and .22 WMR. Cases cannot be reloaded, and rimfire ammunition is more sensitive to being dropped or struck.

Words to get right

Cartridge, round
The complete loaded unit: bullet, case, propellant, primer.
Bullet
Only the projectile. Calling the whole round a bullet is the most common slip.
Caliber
The nominal bore or bullet diameter, in inches or millimeters. A caliber is not a cartridge: ".30 caliber" covers the .30-30, the .308 Winchester, the .30-06 and more.
Grain
The unit for bullet weight and powder charge. 7,000 grains make one pound.
Headstamp
The cartridge name and maker code pressed into the base of the case.

Part 2

What the name tells you, and what it does not

Inch names

.22   .243   .45

The number is the nominal bullet diameter in inches, written without the leading zero: .45 means 0.45 inch. The words after it name the maker or the design: Remington, Winchester, Special, Magnum, ACP (Automatic Colt Pistol).

Metric names

9×19   5.56×45   7.62×39

Nominal bullet diameter × case length, both in millimeters. Two cartridges can share the first number and still fit different rifles: 7.62×39mm feeds an AK, 7.62×51mm feeds a .308 Winchester rifle.

Hyphenated names

.30-06   .30-30

The first number is still the caliber. The second is history: .30-06 is the .30 caliber cartridge adopted in 1906, said "thirty-aught-six"; .30-30 carried 30 grains of smokeless powder in its first load.

What the name says vs. what the bullet measures

The number in a name is nominal. The filled dot is the measured bullet diameter; the hollow dot is the number in the name. Where the two sit apart, the name is a holdover from an older design, a rounding, or a different way of measuring the bore.

The case tells you what it is

Every centerfire case carries a headstamp: the cartridge name and a maker code pressed into the base. Rimfire cases carry only a maker letter, so for .22s the box is the label.

The firearm carries its chambering stamped on the barrel or slide. The rule a safety course wants you to leave with: the headstamp and the barrel stamp match, exactly, before a round goes in. "It fits" proves nothing, as the mismatch section shows.

Part 4

Shotguns count the other way

A shotgun's gauge is not a diameter. It is the number of lead balls of the bore's diameter that together weigh one pound. Twelve such balls make a pound for a 12-gauge, so a smaller gauge number means a bigger bore.

The .410 is the exception: it is a bore diameter in inches, like a rifle caliber. Measured as a gauge it would be about 67.

Shotgun shells are named by gauge and by length (2¾, 3, 3½ inches). The length stamped on the barrel is the longest shell the chamber takes.

Part 5

Bullet construction

The diameter says which firearm a bullet fits. The construction says what the bullet does when it arrives. Most boxes name the construction with a short code.

Part 6

Mismatches a safety course cares about

The one ruleRead the headstamp on the case. Read the chambering on the barrel or slide. Load only when the two match. Fitting the chamber proves nothing.
